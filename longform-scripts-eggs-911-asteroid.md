# VIDEO 3: Why Americans Refrigerate Eggs (and Brits Don't)

### Narration

**[0:00, cold open] VISUAL: split screen: an American fridge with an egg tray, a British kitchen counter with eggs by the kettle.**

> In America, eggs live in the fridge. In Britain, you'll find them on a shelf at room temperature, right there in the supermarket aisle.
>
> Both countries have people who are very sure the other one is wrong. And here's the strange part: they're both doing the sensible thing for their own system.
>
> The difference isn't the egg. It's what happens to the egg before you ever buy it. By the end of this video you'll know exactly what that is, and why an egg bought in London shouldn't be treated like an egg bought in Dallas.

**[0:50, Chapter 1: The invisible coating] VISUAL: macro shot of an eggshell, then an animated cuticle layer over the pores.**

> Start with the shell. It looks solid, but it has thousands of tiny pores. And when a hen lays an egg, it's coated in a thin natural layer called the cuticle, sometimes called the bloom. It works like a seal.
>
> Hold that thought, because what happens to that seal is the whole story.

**[2:00, Chapter 2: The American way] VISUAL: an egg-washing line (use licensed footage or a clearly labeled animation).**

> In the United States, commercial eggs are washed before they're sold. That's the rule, and it cleans the shell.
>
> But washing also takes off the cuticle. Without that seal, the shell is more permeable. So refrigeration steps in as the safeguard: cold slows bacterial growth, and US eggs are kept cold from the farm to your kitchen.
>
> One consequence you may have noticed: a cold egg left on a warm counter can get wet with condensation. Some sources say that moisture can help bacteria move through the shell, which is one reason food-safety guidance says to keep US eggs cold once they've been chilled.

*[**CONFIRMED (secondary summary of the Federal Register):** the refrigeration rule belongs to the **FDA**, not USDA: 21 CFR 118.4(e) requires shell eggs held at or below 45 F ambient starting 36 hours after lay, through storage and transport (final rule published July 9, 2009, 74 FR 33030; a separate FDA rule sets a 45 F retail standard). USDA's role is grading and the washing standards. The USDA wash-water temperature and the 7 CFR 56 wording were NOT confirmed; keep "commercial eggs are washed" as a general statement and cite USDA only for grading. The condensation claim remains a secondary paraphrase of USDA. Safe on-screen line: "FDA rules require eggs be kept at 45 F or below from 36 hours after laying." **Primary check from the FDA "Key Temperatures for Egg Safety" graphic (2020, for food service and retail):** "Refrigerate untreated shell eggs while stored or displayed at 7 C (45 F)." Note the word "untreated": pasteurized shell eggs are treated differently, so say "untreated shell eggs" if citing the graphic.]*

**[3:30, Chapter 3: The British way] VISUAL: British hens in a barn, a red Lion stamp on a shell.**

> Britain does it differently. Eggs there aren't washed, so the cuticle stays on, and the egg keeps its own seal.
>
> That's not just a British habit. Across the European Union, the marketing rules for top-grade, Class A eggs say they must not be washed or cleaned, because washing can damage the shell, which the rules call an effective barrier against bacteria. There's a narrow exception where a country authorized washing before 2003, and those eggs have to be labeled "washed eggs".
>
> But that only works if the hen is healthy to begin with. So since 1998, a scheme called the British Lion Code of Practice has done the work earlier in the chain. Hens are vaccinated against salmonella. Hens, eggs, and feed are traceable. Rodents are strictly controlled. And Lion eggs carry a best-before date stamped on the shell.
>
> It worked well enough that in October 2017, the UK's Food Standards Agency changed its advice. Since the 1988 salmonella scare, pregnant women, babies, and older people had been told to avoid runny or raw eggs. The FSA said British Lion eggs were now safe for them to eat runny, even raw.
>
> Let that sink in. In one country, the advice is: keep every egg cold. In the other, the advice is that a vaccinated hen's egg can be eaten raw by a pregnant woman.
>
> But there's a catch, and it matters. The advice applies to eggs produced under the Lion Code, or an equivalent scheme. Other British eggs, and eggs from outside the UK, should still be cooked through for those groups. And it doesn't cover people who are severely immunocompromised.

**[5:00, Chapter 4: So why doesn't the US switch?] VISUAL: map of vaccinated vs non-vaccinated flocks (use only if sourced; otherwise text on screen).**

> You might wonder why the US doesn't just vaccinate and skip the washing. In 2004, the FDA proposed an egg-safety rule and looked at requiring hen vaccination. According to a veterinary-medicine report, agency officials decided they didn't have enough evidence to require it, and wanted real-world data, not just lab studies. Some US farms vaccinate voluntarily.
>
> There's also an infrastructure explanation sometimes offered: that eggs were traditionally moved through supply chains that weren't refrigerated, so washing and then chilling became the American system.
>
> I want to be careful here. These are explanations from food-safety sites and news coverage, not an official history. What's not in doubt is the core difference: wash and chill in one country, protect the shell and vaccinate in the other.

*[Do NOT quote the UK case counts ("14,000 to 8,000"): one blog source. **FDA 2004 CONFIRMED in part:** the FDA published its proposed rule on Sept 22, 2004 (69 FR 56824) and the preamble discusses a vaccination requirement; the "not enough evidence" conclusion comes from an AVMA (JAVMA News, 2010) report, not the preamble text, which I did not open. Say "decided not to require it" only. Japan: one source says Japan also washes and refrigerates; leave out unless checked.]*

**[6:00, Chapter 5: What to do when you travel] VISUAL: a suitcase, a hotel breakfast, a supermarket shelf.**

> So what should you do? Follow the system you're in.
>
> If you buy eggs in the US, keep them in the fridge. If you're in the UK and your eggs are sold at room temperature and unwashed, storing them in a cool place is the normal practice there.
>
> The one rule that holds in both: once an egg has been chilled, it's better to keep it chilled, because of that condensation problem.

*[The last sentence is from a secondary paraphrase of USDA guidance. Also: UK retailers; check current FSA storage guidance before recording. **[VERIFY]**]*

**[6:40, Close]**

> So American eggs in the fridge, British eggs on the shelf. Different washing, different cuticle, different system, and both countries are following their own logic.
>
> Now I want to know: where do you keep your eggs, and in which country? Tell me in the comments. We're going to read the best ones in a follow-up.

**Do NOT say:** that either country is "wrong" or "dirty"; the UK case-count figures; which US agency owns the refrigeration rule; that viewers should change how they store eggs; that raw eggs are safe in general (the UK advice applies to Lion eggs only).
**Sources to cite on screen:** UK Food Standards Agency announcement (Oct 2017), British Lion Code of Practice (British Egg Industry Council), USDA/FDA shell-egg guidance.
**Thumbnail idea:** an egg split down the middle, a Union Jack shelf on one side, a US fridge on the other; text "WHY?".
**Pinned comment:** "Where are you, and where do you keep your eggs?"

# VIDEO 4: Why 911 Is the Emergency Number

**Working title:** Why 911 Is the Emergency Number (vidIQ 85; low direct search, so build packaging around the story hook: "The first 911 call wasn't an emergency")
**Length as written (measured):** about 570 narrated words, about 3.8 minutes at 150 wpm.

### Narration

**[0:00, cold open] VISUAL: a bright red rotary phone on a desk, in a small-town building.**

> The first 911 call in American history wasn't an emergency. Nobody was hurt. There wasn't a fire or a crime.
>
> It was a politician calling a congressman, on a bright red phone, in a small town in Alabama, on February 16th, 1968.
>
> And that tiny town got there before AT&T, the biggest phone company in the country. This is the story of how, and why the number is 911.

**[1:00, Chapter 1: One country, no number] VISUAL: old phone books, lists of seven-digit numbers for police, fire, ambulance.**

> Before 911, there was no single emergency number in America. If you needed help, you had to know which number to dial for your town's police or fire department. Or you dialed the operator and hoped.
>
> In 1967, a presidential commission on law enforcement recommended a single national emergency number. And the FCC, which regulates phone systems, met with AT&T in November that year to pick one.

*[One source attributes the referral to the FCC to a different commission. Say "a 1967 presidential commission recommended one national number" and nothing about which commission referred it. **[VERIFY]**.]*

**[2:00, Chapter 2: The race] VISUAL: a calendar, Jan 12 to Feb 16, a countdown.**

> On January 12th, 1968, AT&T announced it: 911 would be the universal emergency number. But AT&T's plan covered only the Bell system, and rolling it out nationwide would take time.
>
> Enter a small independent company, the Alabama Telephone Company. Its president, Bob Gallagher, read about AT&T's announcement in the Wall Street Journal, and decided his company would be first.
>
> The system's designer, Robert Fitzgerald, built it in less than a week, according to NPR. And 35 days after AT&T's announcement, it was ready.

*[Setup time resolved: "less than a week" is the build, "35 days" is announcement to first call (Jan 12 to Feb 16). Names are from news sources; B.W. Gallagher in one archive caption. **[VERIFY]** spelling before on-screen titles.]*

**[3:20, Chapter 3: The call] VISUAL: Haleyville, a mayor's office, a police station, the red phone.**

> The town was Haleyville, Alabama. At 2 p.m. on Friday, February 16th, 1968, Alabama House Speaker Rankin Fite sat in the mayor's office and picked up a red phone. He dialed 911.
>
> A short distance away, at the police station, U.S. Representative Tom Bevill answered.
>
> "Hello," Fite is said to have said. It was a ceremonial call, planned for the cameras. And the system worked.
>
> The red phone is still around: it was a gift to the city from the Alabama Telephone Company, and sources say it's on display, with one noting a Smithsonian exhibit. Haleyville's system is still in use.

*[Speaker vs "state senator" varies by outlet; most say House Speaker. Do NOT put quoted dialogue on screen as fact; "said to have said" only if sourced. **[VERIFY]**. Display location of the phone differs by source (city hall, Smithsonian). Leave it as "on display".]*

**[4:20, Chapter 4: Why 911?] VISUAL: three keypad digits lighting up one at a time.**

> So why those three digits? Honestly, the official reasoning isn't well documented, and there's some speculation. But sources give a consistent core.
>
> It was short, so it would be fast in a panic. It was easy to remember. And it hadn't been assigned as an area code or an office code, so it didn't collide with anything. The National Emergency Number Association is quoted as saying 911 best met the phone industry's long-range numbering plans and switching setups of the time.
>
> You'll sometimes hear it was chosen to match 211, 411 and 611, or because of how a rotary dial turns. Those are guesses, and the sources contradict each other, so I'm not going to tell you.

*[**RE-CHECKED, still secondary:** the "short, easy to remember, not already used as an area or office code" core is consistent across sources. The NENA quote ("best met the long-range numbering plans and switching configurations of the telephone industry") is quoted by a secondary site; NENA's own statement was not found. Wikipedia says how 911 was chosen "is not well known and is subject to much speculation", including that it resembles 211/411/611. The rotary-dial and "phantom calls from a faulty hook switch" claims are blog-level and stay out. Keep the "honestly, it isn't well documented" framing, which is the true state of the record.]*

**[5:10, Chapter 5: Spreading] VISUAL: map of early adopters.**

> Others followed within weeks. Nome, Alaska, went live on February 22nd. Huntington, Indiana, got AT&T's first rollout on March 1st.
>
> Then it spread, town by town, over decades.

**[5:50, Chapter 6: What 911 is now] VISUAL: dispatch center, phone map.**

> Today, the United States has more than 5,700 911 call centers, called public safety answering points, covering more than 3,100 counties. The National Emergency Number Association estimates about 240 million calls to 911 every year, and in many areas 80 percent or more come from cell phones.
>
> The FCC's latest fee report, as summarized by one outlet, counted about 213 million voice calls to 911 in 2024 from the states and territories that reported, with about 71 percent from wireless phones. Texts to 911 rose to about 2.2 million, nearly double the year before, but that's still only about one percent of voice calls.

*[**NENA figures CONFIRMED from the NENA 9-1-1 Statistics page you supplied:** "As of February 2021, the United States has 5,748 primary and secondary PSAPs and 3,135 counties"; "An estimated 240 million calls are made to 9-1-1 in the U.S. each year. In many areas, 80% or more are from wireless devices." Also on that page: 93% of counties with 911 coverage have enhanced 911, and 98.9% of the population has some Phase I and Phase II wireless service (that is wireless location capability, not simply "access to 911"). The page's data is dated February 2021, so say "as of 2021" for the PSAP count.]*
*[**PARTLY CONFIRMED:** the FCC numbers (212,910,794 voice calls; 71.3% wireless; about 2.24 million texts, up about 89%; 3,967 text-capable centers) are consistent across several summaries, but all trace to one aggregator citing the FCC's 17th Annual 911 Fee Report; the FCC report itself was not found. The FCC total covers reporting jurisdictions only (about 55 states and territories), which is why NENA's older estimate (about 240 million calls a year) is higher. Say "reported" and attribute each figure. **[VERIFY]** on the FCC site before putting numbers on screen.]*

**[6:40, Close and action]**

> So a number that started as a ceremonial call between two politicians in a small Alabama town is now the number almost every American knows without thinking.
>
> One thing you can do today: find out whether your county accepts texts to 911. It's not available everywhere, and you don't want to find out in the middle of an emergency.
>
> Next time you see a red phone in a museum, you'll know the story.

**Do NOT say:** which commission referred the number to the FCC; any rotary-dial explanation; that the first call was a real emergency; any claim about the whole country having text-to-911.
**Sources to cite on screen:** NENA 9-1-1 statistics, NPR (50 years of 911, 2018), UPI (2018), FCC 911 Fee Report.
**Thumbnail idea:** the red phone, text "NOT AN EMERGENCY".
**Pinned comment:** "Does your county take texts to 911? Check and tell us."

> It's a clear night over New York. A bright light appears in the sky, brighter than anything you've seen, and it moves fast.
>
> A rock about a kilometer wide, travelling at roughly 17 kilometers a second. Faster than a rifle bullet, many times over. From the top of the atmosphere to the ground, it takes seconds.

**[1:30, Chapter 1: How big is a kilometer?] VISUAL: a 1 km sphere next to Manhattan landmarks (use true-to-scale graphics).**

> A kilometer is about 0.6 miles. Standing on the ground, that's roughly a ten-minute walk. Now picture a rock that size, travelling at about 17 kilometers a second.
>
> It doesn't have to be a direct hit on the city to matter. Even a miss by a few miles would be a catastrophe, and the Sandia National Laboratories modeled a roughly 1.4 kilometer asteroid hitting the ocean off New York. In their simulation, the asteroid vaporizes, throws hundreds of cubic miles of superheated water vapor, melted rock, and debris into the upper atmosphere, and sends a shock wave that levels much of New England.

**[2:30, Chapter 2: The energy] VISUAL: a bar chart: the largest nuclear test vs the impact, with a clear scale label.**

>
> For comparison, the largest nuclear weapon ever tested, the Soviet Tsar Bomba, was about 50 megatons. So this is on the order of a thousand times more.

*[Source: Collins, Melosh and Marcus (2005) equations, implemented in `asteroid_impact_model.py` and checked against the paper's own Table 6 example (1.75 km asteroid), where it reproduces the energy, crater, fireball, magnitude, blast and thermal figures. **[3:00, Chapter 3: Second zero] VISUAL: a countdown clock, "T = 0".**

> At impact, a crater opens. On land, the equations give a final crater about 14 kilometers across, nine miles. Manhattan is about 13 miles long; this crater would be most of its width, and then some. Ground shock is equivalent to a magnitude 7.8 earthquake.
>
> Within about 10 kilometers, six miles, the thrown-out rock lands as a layer roughly 100 meters deep, taller than a 30-story building.

*[Crater: 13.9 km (bedrock) or 14.4 km (sedimentary); range about 12-16 km across 12-20 km/s. Magnitude 7.8 (7.6-7.9). Ejecta at 10 km: about 97 m at 17 km/s. Ground zero assumed central Manhattan; the actual site is a script choice.]*

**[3:50, Chapter 4: The first minutes] VISUAL: concentric distance rings over a map of the New York region, one ring appearing per line of narration.**

>
> The equations say the heat would be enough to set clothing on fire out to about 120 kilometers, 75 miles.
