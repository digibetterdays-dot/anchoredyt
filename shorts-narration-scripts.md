# Shorts: Full Narration Scripts

Three Shorts-only topics. Narration is written to be read at about 150 words per minute. Every claim is limited to what the fact-check in `bonus-chapter-production-plan.md` supports. Nothing here uses the dropped stop-sign "from behind" line, the school-bus "1.24x peripheral vision" figure, or any named inventor or year for the ambulance lettering.

Common format: vertical 9:16, first line on screen within 1 second, loop-friendly last line.

## SHORT A: Why AMBULANCE is written backwards (target 32-38s)

**Title:** Why AMBULANCE Is Written Backwards (vidIQ score 92)

**Narration (84 words, about 34s)**

> Why does this ambulance have its name written backwards?
>
> Because that word isn't meant for you. It's meant for the driver in front.
>
> When you look in a rearview mirror, everything is flipped left to right. So the word is flipped first, and then the mirror flips it back. The driver reads it instantly, and moves over.
>
> That one second matters. An ambulance can't wait while someone works out what a word says.
>
> So next time you see one, check your mirror. Notice anything?

| Time | Narration beat | On-screen text | Visual |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0-3s | "Why does this ambulance have its name written backwards?" | `AMBULANCE` (mirrored) | Ambulance front, mirrored lettering, tight crop |
| 3-8s | "Because that word isn't meant for you. It's meant for the driver in front." | `Not for you.` | Pull back to show a car ahead |
| 8-19s | "When you look in a rearview mirror... flips it back." | `Mirror flips it` | Rearview mirror view; the word flips to normal as the mirror "wipes" across |
| 19-26s | "The driver reads it instantly, and moves over." | `Instant read` | Car pulls aside, ambulance passes |
| 26-32s | "That one second matters... what a word says." | `Every second counts` | Lights flashing |
| 32-36s | "So next time you see one, check your mirror. Notice anything?" | `Check your mirror` | Cut to a driver's mirror view. Hold on the readable word to loop to the start |

**Do not say:** who started it, when, or in which country. The origin is unclear.
**Safe caption:** "Mirror-image lettering so drivers read it correctly in their rearview mirror."
**Pinned comment:** "Fun fact: the lettering is flipped on the front only, because that's the side drivers see in their mirror."  *(Check this against real vehicles before posting; it matches the reasoning but is not sourced.)*

## SHORT B: Why school buses are that exact yellow (target 45-52s)

**Title:** Why School Buses Are That Exact Yellow (vidIQ score 85 as a long-form title; rescore as a Short before publishing)

**Narration (129 words, about 52s; trim a line if it must stay under 50s)**

> Every school bus in America is the same yellow. That's no accident.
>
> Before 1939, school buses came in whatever color the maker or the local district chose. Then a professor at Teachers College in Columbia, Frank Cyr, brought state officials and engineers together to write the first national school bus standards.
>
> One big question was color. He laid out a range of shades, from lemon yellow through to deep orange.
>
> The group picked one, and it got an official name: National School Bus Chrome. The reasons were simple. You can spot it from far away, and black lettering stands out against it.
>
> Cyr himself reportedly thought it looked more orange than yellow.
>
> So that color isn't a style. It's a standard, chosen on purpose, more than eighty years ago.

| Time | Narration beat | On-screen text | Visual |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0-4s | "Every school bus in America is the same yellow. That's no accident." | `Same yellow. On purpose.` | Row of buses, then a single bus close-up |
| 4-14s | "Before 1939... first national school bus standards." | `1939` | Vintage buses in mixed colors (use clearly labeled archive or AI-generated stand-ins; do not imply a real photo) |
| 14-22s | "One big question was color... deep orange." | `Lemon to orange` | Swatch strip, yellows to orange |
| 22-34s | "The group picked one... black lettering stands out against it." | `National School Bus Chrome` | One swatch highlights; black "SCHOOL BUS" lettering over it |
| 34-40s | "Cyr himself reportedly thought it looked more orange than yellow." | `"More orange than yellow"` | Swatch next to an orange for comparison |
| 40-48s | "So that color isn't a style... eighty years ago." | `A standard, not a style` | Bus driving away at sunrise; hold for loop |

**Facts used, and their status**
- April 1939 conference at Teachers College, Columbia, organized by Frank W. Cyr: sourced, consistent.
- First national school-bus standards, including color: sourced. Count of standards differs by source, so none is quoted.
- A range of shades from yellow to orange was displayed; "National School Bus Chrome" chosen for visibility and contrast with black lettering: sourced.
- Cyr thought it closer to orange: "reportedly", from one account; hedge kept.
- "More than eighty years ago": 1939 to 2026, safe.
- "Before 1939, whatever color": **soft claim.** Sources say standards did not exist before the conference. If you want zero risk, change to "Before 1939, there was no national standard."

**Left out on purpose:** the "1.24x better in peripheral vision" figure (no original study found), the formula-on-file claim, the dollar amount, the number of states, and the Minnesota holdout.

## SHORT C: Why stop signs are octagons (target 38-45s)

**Title:** The Real Story Behind the 8-Sided Stop Sign (vidIQ score 90, from title generation; strip its hashtags into the description)

**Narration (97 words, about 39s)**

> Stop signs weren't always red. Or eight-sided.
>
> Early stop signs were yellow. Red paint that wouldn't fade wasn't available yet, so red came later.
>
> Then, in 1922, a committee of state highway officials set out to standardize road signs, and gave the stop sign a shape no other sign used: the octagon.
>
> The thinking was simple. A unique shape tells you what a sign says before you can read it, even at night, when early signs weren't reflective.
>
> So when you stop at that corner, you're obeying a shape designed to be unmistakable, a hundred years ago.

| Time | Narration beat | On-screen text | Visual |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0-3s | "Stop signs weren't always red. Or eight-sided." | `Not always red.` | Modern stop sign, color drains to yellow |
| 3-12s | "Early stop signs were yellow... red came later." | `Yellow first` | Yellow-toned sign. Label any illustration as a recreation |
| 12-24s | "Then, in 1922... the octagon." | `1922: the octagon` | Silhouettes of road signs of many shapes; only the octagon stays |
| 24-35s | "The thinking was simple... weren't reflective." | `Shape beats words` | Night road, signs as silhouettes |
| 35-42s | "So when you stop at that corner... a hundred years ago." | `Designed to be unmistakable` | Stop sign at a corner, holds for loop |

**Facts used, and their status**
- Yellow early signs because fade-resistant red was unavailable: sourced (one source).
- 1922 committee with AASHO standardized the octagon: sourced. Sources vary on the committee wording, so the narration says "a committee of state highway officials" (AASHO's members). Check wording against the MUTCD or AASHTO history if you want it exact.
- Shape identifiable at night because early signs were not reflective: sourced. Phrased as "the thinking" to stay honest about the source level.
- "A hundred years ago": 1922 to 2026 is 104 years; "a hundred years ago" is a safe round figure.

**Removed per your decision:** any claim that the octagon is recognizable from behind. **Also not used:** William Phelps Eno and the 1914/1915 Michigan dates, because the source years conflict. If you want an Eno beat, add: "The idea of a stop sign goes back to a writer named William Phelps Eno" (one source credits him with the concept, 1900).

## Production notes for all three

1. **Rescore titles as Shorts.** Only #A and #C were scored as short-form. Score #B as `short` before publishing.
2. **Voice:** one consistent narrator. Use short sentences, a short pause (about 0.4s) before each reveal.
3. **Imagery honesty:** where a real photo or footage doesn't exist (1939 buses, early yellow stop signs), use clearly illustrated recreations. Do not present AI imagery as archival.
4. **Pair each Short with its long-form sibling only where one exists.** A, B and C are Shorts-only, so end-screens should point to the channel's long-form playlist (Yellowstone, eggs, Cascadia) instead.
5. **Soft claims to spot-check once before recording:** the pinned-comment line in A, the "before 1939" line in B, and the "state highway officials" wording in C.
