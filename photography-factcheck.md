## The owner's draft paragraph, claim by claim

| Claim | Verdict | What the sources say |
|---|---|---|
| "In 1856 the king of Naples banned photography, possibly because of the evil eye." | **Reported prohibition: use, attributed. Evil eye: not a confirmed reason.** | **Owner-supplied (Nov 5):** a published history of photography reports that in 1856 the King of Naples (Ferdinand II, Kingdom of the Two Sicilies, capital Naples) forbade photography in his territories, and says the reason was not known. **Title and page of that history still to note here.** * A period record (London Stereoscopic Company catalogue, 1856, via a transcription) separately records a prohibition on "any more Photographic pictures of Pompeii", no reason given. He had **allowed** photography across the kingdom in 1847 (Bridges's letter to Talbot); a Russian photographer worked at Pompeii in 1859 under a special royal permit. The evil-eye link comes only from the unsourced 2006 *Awake!* article: script presents it as a guess. |
| Delaroche: "From today painting is dead!" | **Almost certainly apocryphal. Script wording (owner): "A famous quotation later attributed to painter Paul Delaroche declared that painting was dead."** | The line first appears in print 30+ years later (1873 per Quote Investigator, or Gaston Tissandier's *Les merveilles de la photographie*, 1874: sources disagree on which). In 1839 Delaroche actually wrote a report on the daguerreotype that Arago read out, praising its precision and saying it would help train painters (Gernsheim quotes him calling it "an immense service to the arts"). He kept painting until he died in 1856. Scholar Stephen Bann says the quote froze the painting/photography story in "mythic stasis". |
| Painters "saw it as a threat to their livelihood." | **Use, in general terms (owner framing).** | Before photography, paintings and drawings were among the main ways of recording faces, places and events; photography gave portrait painters a new competitor, since people could have a photograph made instead of commissioning a painting. Documented voice: Baudelaire, *Salon of 1859*. |
| "Photography's stringent truth to optical reality could nullify the individual's apprehension of beauty." | **Cut.** | Only source found is the same *Awake!* article, quoting an unnamed "commentator". No author, no date. Don't use it as a quote. |
| Photos "shattered cherished illusions of beauty and youth." | **Use as the owner's general framing, not as a quote.** | Painted portraits could idealise (soften wrinkles, adjust proportions, improve posture, follow conventions of beauty); a photograph recorded detail an artist might leave out. No claim about how sitters felt (unsourced). |

## Sitters and the camera (added Nov 5, for the Shorts)
Search-level sources: Getty "Focus: Daguerreotypes" gallery text (lack of colour left many sitters frustrated with their grey complexions); Carol Shloss, "The Inconstant Daguerreotype", p. 163 (subjects' "common disappointment" with their images), cited in a UConn dissertation that also calls the frozen likeness "uncanny and even off-putting"; 1839 New York Observer praising portraits made "not with man's feeble, false, and flattering pencil"; long exposures, metal head rests, fixed stares. No direct 1840s quote from a disappointed sitter found yet.

## Who was the first photographer?

**Joseph Nicéphore Niépce**, with a careful wording:
- *View from the Window at Le Gras*, Saint-Loup-de-Varennes, France. Dated **1827** by the Harry Ransom Center (Austin, Texas), which holds it; many sources say "1826 or 1827". The Ransom Center calls it the **earliest surviving photograph made with a camera** (it renamed its display from "The First Photograph" to "The Niépce Heliograph" in 2019). Pewter plate coated with bitumen of Judea; exposure: **about 8 hours** is the traditional estimate (Helmut Gernsheim, from sunlight on both sides of the courtyard); **several days** ("three or more") per Jean-Louis Marignier (Université Paris-Sud), who recreated the process and studied Niépce's letters; the Ransom Center cites the longer estimate (via Smarthistory). Don't confuse with the c. 1832 physautotype (about 8 hours in sun). *
- Earlier: **Thomas Wedgwood** (son of the potter) made light images around 1800 and published in 1802 with Humphry Davy, but could not **fix** them: they darkened away in daylight. So: first attempts, Wedgwood; earliest surviving camera photograph, Niépce; first practical public process, Daguerre.

## Daguerre and 1839
- **7 January 1839:** François Arago announces Daguerre's process to the Académie des Sciences, without the details. (One Wikipedia page says 9 January; most sources say 7.)
- **1 August 1839:** King Louis-Philippe signs the law for the state to buy the process; lifetime pensions go to Daguerre (6,000 francs) and Niépce's heir (4,000 francs).
- **19 August 1839:** full method made public at a joint meeting of the Académies des Sciences and des Beaux-Arts, presented as France's gift "free to the world". Britain was the exception: Daguerre's agent patented it there days before.
- Niépce had died in 1833; Daguerre had been his partner since 1829. (Secondary; widely stated.) *
- *Boulevard du Temple* (Daguerre, 1838) is often called the first photograph of a person: a man having his shoes shined, the only figure still long enough to register. (Secondary; widely stated.) *

## How painting changed
- **Delacroix** was a founding member of the Société héliographique (1851), the first society devoted to photography; he drew from photographs made with Eugène Durieu and collected photographs (Musée Delacroix exhibition, 2008).
- **Baudelaire** posed for photographers despite his 1859 attack (Étienne Carjat portrait, c. 1863, Met Museum).
- **First Impressionist exhibition:** 15 April to 15 May 1874, 35 boulevard des Capucines, Paris, in the studio of the photographer **Nadar**; 30 artists, 165 works. The name "Impressionist" came from Louis Leroy's review in *Le Charivari*, 25 April 1874. Monet painted *Boulevard des Capucines* from that building.

## Before photography
Portrait painting and miniatures for those who could pay; silhouettes and profile-tracing machines (the physiognotrace, 18th century) for those who couldn't; the camera obscura, whose image could only be traced by hand. (General; widely documented.)

## Owner decision (Nov 5)
The script does not name the king; it says "the king of Naples". (Ferdinand II reigned 1830-1859, so it would be him, but naming him adds nothing the claim needs.)

## Do NOT say
"The king banned photography" as fact (say: a published history reports he forbade it in 1856, reason unknown); "because of the evil eye" (a guess, never a fact); Delaroche "said" the line (say: is credited with); "the first photograph ever" (say: earliest surviving photograph made with a camera); the "stringent truth" quote.
