# When Photography Terrified the World: fact check

Checked Nov 5 2026 by web search. Primary-source pages (Quote Investigator, Harry Ransom Center) are blocked from the cloud session, so most items below rest on **secondary sources** (search summaries of museum, encyclopedia and scholarly pages). ## The owner's draft paragraph, claim by claim

| Claim | Verdict | What the sources say |
|---|---|---|
| "In 1856 the king of Naples banned photography, possibly because it was thought to be associated with the evil eye." | **Not supported as stated.** | The sentence comes from a 2006 *Awake!* article ("How Photography Saw the Light"), which gives no source and itself hedges with "possibly". A period record (London Stereoscopic Company catalogue, 1856, via a transcription) says only that "The King of Naples has prohibited the taking of any more Photographic pictures of Pompeii" (Pompeii only, no reason given). The king was Ferdinand II. He had **allowed** photography across the kingdom in 1847 (Bridges's letter to Talbot), and a Russian photographer still worked at Pompeii in 1859 under a special royal permit. Belief in the evil eye (*jettatura*) was real in Bourbon Naples, but **no source links it to photography**. |
| Delaroche: "From today painting is dead!" | **Almost certainly apocryphal.** | The line first appears in print 30+ years later (1873 per Quote Investigator, or Gaston Tissandier's *Les merveilles de la photographie*, 1874: sources disagree on which). In 1839 Delaroche actually wrote a report on the daguerreotype that Arago read out, praising its precision and saying it would help train painters (Gernsheim quotes him calling it "an immense service to the arts"). He kept painting until he died in 1856. Scholar Stephen Bann says the quote froze the painting/photography story in "mythic stasis". |
| Painters "saw it as a threat to their livelihood." | **Fair, if kept general.** | Widely stated. Best documented voice: Baudelaire, *Salon of 1859*: photography was "the refuge of every would-be painter, every painter too ill-endowed or too lazy to complete his studies". Use that instead. |
| "Photography's stringent truth to optical reality could nullify the individual's apprehension of beauty." | **Cut.** | Only source found is the same *Awake!* article, quoting an unnamed "commentator". No author, no date. Don't use it as a quote. |
| Photos "shattered cherished illusions of beauty and youth." | **Cut as a claim.** | Same unsourced article. The idea (sitters disliking unflattering photos) is plausible but unsourced here. |

## Who was the first photographer?

**Joseph Nicéphore Niépce**, with a careful wording:
- *View from the Window at Le Gras*, Saint-Loup-de-Varennes, France. Dated **1827** by the Harry Ransom Center (Austin, Texas), which holds it; many sources say "1826 or 1827". The Ransom Center calls it the **earliest surviving photograph made with a camera** (it renamed its display from "The First Photograph" to "The Niépce Heliograph" in 2019). Pewter plate coated with bitumen of Judea; exposure estimates range from about 8 hours to several days. *
- Earlier: **Thomas Wedgwood** (son of the potter) made light images around 1800 and published in 1802 with Humphry Davy, but could not **fix** them: they darkened away in daylight. So: first attempts, Wedgwood; earliest surviving camera photograph, Niépce; first practical public process, Daguerre.

## Daguerre and 1839
- **7 January 1839:** François Arago announces Daguerre's process to the Académie des Sciences, without the details. (One Wikipedia page says 9 January; most sources say 7.)
- **1 August 1839:** King Louis-Philippe signs the law for the state to buy the process; lifetime pensions go to Daguerre (6,000 francs) and Niépce's heir (4,000 francs).
- **19 August 1839:** full method made public at a joint meeting of the Académies des Sciences and des Beaux-Arts, presented as France's gift "free to the world". Britain was the exception: Daguerre's agent patented it there days before.
- Niépce had died in 1833; Daguerre had been his partner since 1829. (Secondary; widely stated.) *
- *Boulevard du Temple* (Daguerre, 1838) is often called the first photograph of a person: a man having his shoes shined, the only figure still long enough to register. (Secondary; widely stated.) *

## How painting changed
- **Delacroix** was a founding member of the Société héliographique (1851), the first society devoted to photography; he drew from photographs made with Eugène Durieu and collected photographs (Musée Delacroix exhibition, 2008).
- **Baudelaire** posed for photographers despite his 1859 attack (Étienne Carjat portrait, c. 1863, Met Museum).
- **First Impressionist exhibition:** 15 April to 15 May 1874, 35 boulevard des Capucines, Paris, in the studio of the photographer **Nadar**; 30 artists, 165 works. The name "Impressionist" came from Louis Leroy's review in *Le Charivari*, 25 April 1874. Monet painted *Boulevard des Capucines* from that building.

## Before photography
Portrait painting and miniatures for those who could pay; silhouettes and profile-tracing machines (the physiognotrace, 18th century) for those who couldn't; the camera obscura, whose image could only be traced by hand. (General; widely documented.)

## Do NOT say
"The king of Naples banned photography" (say: banned photographing Pompeii, per one 1856 record); "because of the evil eye" (no source); Delaroche "said" the line (say: is credited with); "the first photograph ever" (say: earliest surviving photograph made with a camera); the "stringent truth" quote.
