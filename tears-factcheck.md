# Why Do Humans Cry?: fact check

Checked Nov 6 2026 by web search (abstracts, repository pages and review papers; one full-text PDF host was blocked in the cloud session). The owner's draft was mostly right; corrections are marked **FIXED**.

| Claim in draft | Status | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Three kinds of tears: basal, reflex, emotional | Textbook-level; standard classification. | |
| Blinking spreads the tear film; thousands of blinks a day | Textbook-level (about 15-20 blinks a minute while awake). | |
| Reflex tears from dust, smoke, wind, onion; watering when yawning or laughing hard | Textbook-level. | |
| Frey 1981: emotional tears about 24% more protein than onion tears | **FIXED wording.** Frey, DeSota-Johnson, Hoffman & McCall, "Effect of stimulus on the chemical composition of human tears", *American Journal of Ophthalmology* 92(4):559-567 (1981). Abstract: "The protein concentration of emotional tears from women exceeded that of irritant-induced tears by 24%." The comparison was in women. Script now says "in women's tears" and drops the unverified quote "even Frey said scientists don't really know"; it says instead that nobody is sure what the difference means. | EPA HERO record of the paper |
| Tears drain at the inner corner into a channel and into the nose; crying hard makes the nose run | Textbook-level (puncta, canaliculi, lacrimal sac, nasolacrimal duct). | |
| Newborns make some tears from birth but not enough to spill; visible tears usually at 1-3 months | Confirmed by pediatricians quoted in parenting press (Children's Health, Dallas, via The Bump: "between 1 and 3 months"; Vinmec: 1 to 3 months). No AAP page found. Ranges elsewhere: 2 weeks to 3 months. Script: "for the first weeks" (not "two weeks"), "usually somewhere between one and three months". | The Bump; Vinmec |
| 7,000+ people, 41 countries, photos with digitally added tears, more willingness to support | Confirmed. Zickfeld et al., "Tears evoke the intention to offer social support: A systematic investigation of the interpersonal effects of emotional crying across 41 countries", *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology* 95:104137 (2021), registered report. 7,007 participants, 41 countries, effect d = 0.49. | ScienceDirect; Tilburg repository |
| 1980s surveys: 85% of women, 73% of men felt better after crying | **FIXED wording.** Frey et al. (1983), "Crying behavior in the human adult": 85% of women and 73% of men said they *generally* felt better after crying (a trait question). In the same study's diary of actual episodes, mood improved after only about 40% of them. Script: "In one study from the early 1980s, when people were asked how they usually feel after crying..." | Bylsma et al. 2011 (*J Research in Personality*), describing Frey 1983 |
| 5,000+ students, 35 countries, most recent cry: most better, a third no change, one in ten worse | **FIXED numbers.** Bylsma, Vingerhoets & Rottenberg, "When is crying cathartic? An international study", *Journal of Social and Clinical Psychology* 27(10):1165-1187 (2008): 5,096 students (2,181 men, 2,915 women) in 35 countries. Mood after the most recent cry: 51.4% better, 38.3% the same, 9.7% worse (figures as cited in a Hrčak review; the original PDF host was blocked). Script: "about half felt better, close to four in ten felt no change, and about one in ten felt worse." | USF Digital Commons abstract; Hrčak review "Crying and Health" |
| Social support predicted feeling better; shame or negative responses predicted feeling worse | Confirmed, same 2008 paper (support, resolution, positive events -> better; negative responses, embarrassment or shame, witnessing others' suffering -> worse). | same |
| Holding back tears not proven harmful | Consistent with the reviews (Rottenberg, Bylsma & Vingerhoets 2008, "Is crying beneficial?", *Current Directions in Psychological Science* 17:400-404: survey and lab evidence disagree; health claims not established). Framed as "science hasn't proven that". | SAGE |

## Added Nov 6 (owner asked to extend past 8 minutes)
| Claim | Status | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Happy tears: overwhelming positive feelings often shown with an expression of the opposite emotion (tears); early evidence that it helps bring the emotion back down | Confirmed. Aragón, Clark, Dyer & Bargh (Yale), "Dimorphous expressions of positive emotion", *Psychological Science* 26(3):259-273 (2015): extremely positive experiences "often generate both positive expressions (e.g., smiles) and expressions normatively reserved for negative emotions (e.g., tears)"; "preliminary evidence of their function as regulators of emotion". Script says "early evidence". | APS journal page; Yale lab PDF |
| Other animals make tears for lubrication and irritation; no clear evidence of emotional tears in any other species; survey of vets and zookeepers found none | Confirmed. Vingerhoets & Bylsma review: "only humans cry with tears in response to emotional events"; "a survey among veterinarians, zookeepers, and other professionals who work with animals yielded no evidence of emotional tears in any other animal species (Frey, 1985)". Gracanin et al. 2018 (*Human Nature*) agree; anecdotes (elephants, macaques) unverified. Script: "As far as science can tell". | Vingerhoets 2013, *Why Only Humans Weep* (OUP); Gracanin et al. 2018 |
| Vingerhoets: tears grew out of young animals' distress calls, shifting to a silent close-range signal; "we cry because we need other people" | Confirmed as his hypothesis (presented as "an idea"). | TIME (2016) interview; Vingerhoets 2013 |
| Women cry about 2-5 times a month, men about once or less | Confirmed as review ranges: "women 2 - 5 times per month, men .5 - 1 time per month" (Vingerhoets & Scheirs 2000, cited in Vingerhoets & Bylsma). The popular "5.3 vs 1.4" (Frey 1983) could not be confirmed for men (one source gives 1.3), so not used. | Vingerhoets & Bylsma review |

## Added Nov 6, second pass (owner's notes: triggers, joy, contagion, nonverbal language, holding back tears, the book *Adult Crying*)
| Claim | Status | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Triggers include grief, frustration, physical or psychological pain; relief, pride and achievement bring tears too | Consistent with Vingerhoets' model of adult crying (helplessness, loss, pain, and positive and moving events). Happy tears as above (Aragón 2015). | Vingerhoets, Cornelius, Van Heck & Becht, "Adult crying: A model and review of the literature", *Review of General Psychology* 4(4):354 (2000) |
| Crying is contagious | **Narrowed to what is shown:** contagious crying in infants. Simner (1971): newborns about 3 days old cried most to another newborn's cry, more than to white noise, a synthetic cry or an older infant's cry. Geangu et al. (2010): also at 1, 3, 6 and 9 months. Debate: Ruffman et al. argue it may be a reaction to the sound, not empathy (said in the script). No solid source found for adults "catching" tears, so not claimed. | Geangu et al. 2010, *Infant Behavior and Development*; Vreden et al. 2025, *Developmental Science* (PMC11733258) |
| Crying is a powerful nonverbal language | Supported as framing by the 41-country tear study (Zickfeld 2021) and Vingerhoets' "we cry because we need other people". Script: "a kind of language that needs no words". | as above |
| "Crying is a useful outlet, and systematically holding back tears may damage our health" | **NOT supported; kept as a belief.** The phrase matches a 2014 Awake! (JW.org) article, which itself adds that the benefits "have not been scientifically verified". Vingerhoets (editor of *Adult Crying*, 2001, with Cornelius) and Bylsma, "Crying and health: popular and scientific conceptions", *Psihologijske teme* 16(2):275-296 (2007): on crying inhibition and health risk "there is a lack of sound studies"; crying frequency vs self-reported health correlated 0.00 (Vingerhoets et al. 1993). The book's chapters "Crying and health" (Vingerhoets & Scheirs) and "Crying and catharsis" (Cornelius) are cited by later work as showing no faster recovery after crying. Script presents the outlet/holding-back idea as a popular belief and says science hasn't proven it. | Hrčak (open access); Hendriks et al. 2008 (Springer) |
| 140 years of magazine articles: 94% said crying is good for you, many warned against holding it in | Confirmed. Cornelius (1986): 72 popular articles, 1848-1985; "94% promoted crying as beneficial" (as summarised in Rottenberg, Bylsma & Vingerhoets 2008, "Is crying beneficial?"; TIME 2016). | ResearchGate (Rottenberg et al. 2008); TIME |

## Added Nov 6, third pass (owner picked from the candidate list)
| Claim | Status | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Tear film in layers: mucus against the eye, watery middle (most of it), thin oil on top from glands along the eyelid edges, slowing evaporation | Confirmed. NIH StatPearls, "Biochemistry, Tear Film" (NBK572136): "classically divided into three distinct layers – inner mucin, middle aqueous, and outer lipid layer", with overlap in practice; lipid from the meibomian glands; the lipid layer delays evaporation (IntechOpen "The Physiology of Tear Film"). | StatPearls; IntechOpen |

**Owner's notes NOT used, and why**
- "Crying activates the anterior cingulate cortex, the region that processes physical pain": not verified for crying; the social-pain imaging work (rejection) is a different question and the pain overlap is debated.
- "Catharsis undermined by Bushman et al. 2002": that study is about venting anger, not crying; the crying mechanism (support) is already in from Bylsma 2008.
- "Crying frequency peaks in adolescence": the reviews say the opposite (frequency falls from childhood through adolescence to about age 20-25; Vingerhoets & Bylsma 2016; Hastrup et al. 2001 in *Adult Crying*).
- "About a third have cried after sex": the post-coital dysphoria studies (Schweitzer et al.) measure low mood, not crying, with different figures; also off-brand for a general audience.

**Owner's line added (Ch. 7):** "Crying frequency is not a measure of mental health. Some people cry often and are fine. Some rarely cry and are fine. This isn't a diagnosis. It's a question to sit with."

## Wording fix (not a fact)
S03 "even reading this sentence would get uncomfortable" -> "and even just keeping them open would get uncomfortable" (it is a voiceover).

## Do NOT say
"Crying flushes toxins/stress hormones out" (not in the 1981 paper); "emotional tears are chemically proven different" (one old study, women only); "most people feel better after crying" without the caveat; "holding tears in makes you ill" (present it only as a popular belief); "crying is contagious" for adults (only infant contagious crying is shown); "babies can't make tears".
