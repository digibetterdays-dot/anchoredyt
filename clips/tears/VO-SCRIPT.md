## Opening

You're watching a movie. Nobody has hurt you, nothing is in your eyes, and the story isn't even real. And yet your eyes start to sting, and a tear runs down your cheek.

Why? Why does a story on a screen make a human body leak salt water?

Tonight we're following a single tear, from the moment it's made to the moment it lands. And by the end, you'll know why crocodiles cry at dinner, and how a tear you can't even smell can calm an angry man.

## Chapter 1: The three kinds of tears

Start with a simple fact. You are producing tears right now. Not emotional tears: a thin, constant layer that coats the front of your eyes every second you're awake.

Scientists split tears into three kinds. The first are basal tears, the ones that are always there. And they're built in layers, like a tiny sandwich: mucus against the eye to help them stick, a watery middle, and a thin layer of oil on top, from glands along your eyelids, to slow the drying. The watery part even carries proteins that fight bacteria. Every blink spreads a fresh coat, like a windshield wiper with the washer fluid built in.

The second kind are reflex tears, your eye's emergency wash. Dust, smoke, wind, or a chopped onion. The onion is a clever one: a whole onion can't make you cry. Cutting it starts a chain reaction that releases a gas, and when it reaches your eyes, the nerves there raise the alarm, and the glands flood the eye to wash it away.

Then there's the third kind, the strange one: emotional tears. Grief. Frustration. Pain, of the body or of the mind. A goodbye at an airport. Humans cry when feelings get too big to hold.

And it isn't only sadness. People cry with relief, with pride, at weddings, when a team finally wins. Psychologists at Yale found that when a good feeling becomes overwhelming, we often show it with the opposite expression, and their early evidence suggests it helps bring the feeling back down to a level we can handle.

## Chapter 2: Where tears go

So where does a tear go once it's made? In the inner corner of each eye, near your nose, there's a tiny opening. Tears drain into it, down a small channel, and end up inside your nose.

That's why crying makes your nose run. When you cry hard, your eyes make more than that little drain can handle. Some spills down your cheeks. The rest pours into your nose. So the tissue you reach for during a sad movie is really catching tears.

All of this depends on gravity. On the space station in 2013, astronaut Chris Hadfield squeezed water into his eye as if he were crying. It didn't run down his face. It just sat there, a growing ball of water. In his words: your eyes will definitely cry in space, but tears don't fall.

## Chapter 3: Why newborns cry without tears

Now here's something that surprises a lot of new parents. A newborn baby can scream, turn red, and cry with everything they've got, and there's not a single tear on their face.

It's normal. Babies make some tears from birth, enough to keep their eyes moist, but not enough to spill over. Visible tears usually show up somewhere between one and three months of age.

So for those first weeks, a baby's cry is pure sound. Long before we can speak, crying is how we say: I'm hungry. I'm cold. I need you. Crying starts as communication, and in a way, it never stops being that.

Babies even seem to catch crying from each other. In a classic study from 1971, newborns just a few days old cried more when they heard another newborn crying than when they heard an artificial cry, or plain noise. Researchers still debate whether that's early empathy or just a reaction to the sound. But either way, crying spreads.

## Chapter 4: Only humans?

Here's another strange thing. Plenty of animals make tears. Dogs, cats and horses all need them to keep their eyes wet. But in one survey of veterinarians, zookeepers and other people who work with animals, nobody could point to a clear case of an animal crying from feelings.

And then there are crocodiles. For centuries, people said crocodiles weep while they eat their prey. A travel book from the 1300s spread the story, and that's why fake sorrow is still called crocodile tears. In 2007, researchers finally checked. They filmed caimans and alligators, close relatives of crocodiles, while they ate. Five of the seven teared up during their meal. So the legend is true, just not for the reason people thought. Those are reflex tears, probably squeezed out by all that jaw movement. The crocodile isn't sorry at all. And humans can have a version of it too. After some facial nerve injuries, the nerve can regrow along the wrong path, and a person's eyes water every time they eat. Doctors call it crocodile tears syndrome.

As far as science can tell, emotional tears are a human thing. The Dutch psychologist Ad Vingerhoets, who has studied crying for decades, thinks our tears grew out of the loud distress calls young animals make, and became a quiet signal only the people close to us can see.

His short version: we cry because we need other people.

## Chapter 5: Tears as a signal

Scientists have tested this. In one huge study, researchers showed more than 7,000 people in 41 countries photos of faces. Some had tears added digitally; otherwise the photos were identical.

The result: people were more willing to offer support to the faces with tears. Across cultures, a tear seems to say, come closer. It's a kind of language that needs no words.

And tears may send a message you can't even see. In 2023, scientists at the Weizmann Institute in Israel had men sniff either women's emotional tears or salt water. The tears had no smell anyone could notice. Then they played a game designed to provoke them. After sniffing the tears, their aggressive behavior in the game dropped by about 44 percent. It's a small study, but it suggests that tears carry a chemical signal, too.

## Chapter 6: Does crying make you feel better?

But does crying actually make you feel better? Ask people, and most say yes. In one study from the early 1980s, 85 percent of women and 73 percent of men said they usually feel better after crying.

Here's the catch. When researchers asked more than 5,000 students in 35 countries about their most recent cry, about half felt better. Close to four in ten felt no change. And about one in ten actually felt worse.

So what made the difference? One of the biggest factors was other people. Those who got support, someone who listened or sat with them, were the most likely to feel better. People who felt ashamed, or who were brushed off, often didn't.

And the relief may simply take time. In a 2015 experiment, people who cried at sad films felt worse right after. Twenty minutes later, they were back to normal. And after ninety minutes, they felt better than before the film started. So if a cry doesn't help right away, give it a little while.

## Chapter 7: Is holding back tears harmful?

And what about holding tears back? You've probably heard that crying is a healthy outlet, and that bottling up your tears can damage your health. It's one of the oldest ideas about crying. One researcher looked at 140 years of magazine articles about tears: 94 percent said crying was good for you, and many warned against holding it in.

But when scientists reviewed the actual research, including the editor of a major book called Adult Crying, they found there simply aren't enough good studies to show it. In one study, how often people cried had no link at all with how healthy they said they were.

People really do differ. Women cry somewhere around two to five times a month, men about once a month or less, and the range from person to person is huge. But crying frequency is not a measure of mental health. Some people cry often and are fine. Some rarely cry and are fine. What seems to matter more is how people handle the feelings underneath. This isn't a diagnosis. It's a question to sit with.

## Chapter 8: The journey of a tear

So let's follow that movie-night tear one last time. Your eyes were already making tears to protect themselves. Your feelings sent a flood. It spilled past the little drain by your nose, down your cheek, and maybe someone next to you noticed, and moved a little closer.

Science hasn't solved every mystery about tears. But they clearly do more than show sadness. They protect your eyes, wash out what hurts them, and send a message words sometimes can't.

So next time you see someone crying, try a simple question: "Are you okay? Can I help?"
