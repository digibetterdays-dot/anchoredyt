Your brain becomes
highly active.
In some ways,
its electrical activity
resembles the patterns
of wakefulness.
And this is
the stage where
vivid dreams are
especially common.
Yet while your
brain is busy
creating dreams,
most of your
voluntary muscles are
temporarily switched off.
Why?
Imagine dreaming that
you're running from
a dangerous animal.
If your body
followed every movement
in your dream,
you might actually
start running around
your bedroom.
During REM sleep,
the brainstem activates
pathways that temporarily
block most movement
in your skeletal
muscles.
This protective state
is called REM
atonia.
It helps keep
you from acting
out your dreams.
It's almost as
if your brain
is watching an
elaborate movie,
while keeping your
body out of
the action.
