# What Happens If a Nuclear Bomb Goes Off? Second by Second, Then Years Later: voiceover script

Calm, steady, never dramatic: a guide, not a thriller. Slow down on the survival lines ("get inside, stay inside, stay tuned", "almost entirely preventable") and on the Hiroshima ending. **Leave about 2.5 seconds of silence at every chapter break**: the countdown clock and chapter card play there. Pronunciations: kiloton "KILL-uh-tun", Hiroshima "here-oh-SHEE-mah", Nagasaki "nah-gah-SAH-kee".

## Opening

Imagine an ordinary afternoon. You're at work, or at school, or stuck in traffic. And somewhere a few miles away, a nuclear bomb goes off.

What would actually happen to you? Not in a movie. In real life, second by second.

Most of what people believe about this comes from films, and a lot of it is wrong. Some of it is dangerously wrong, because the right move in the first hour can be the difference between life and death.

So tonight, we'll walk through it on a clock: the first second, the first minute, the first hour, the first days, and the years after. We'll use what emergency planners use: a single, small nuclear device, about 10 kilotons, exploding at ground level. And we'll stick to the evidence: government planning guidance, and what really happened in Hiroshima and Nagasaki.

This is about what happens, and what helps. Not about weapons, and not about politics.

## Chapter 1: The first second

It starts with light.

The flash is so intense that it can blind people for a while, even if they aren't looking at it. Light bouncing off buildings and the sky is enough. In daylight, this flash blindness usually lasts seconds, up to about two minutes. At night, when your pupils are wide open, it lasts longer.

Looking straight at the fireball is worse. It can burn the retina at the back of the eye and leave a permanent blind spot, and for a bomb this size, that can happen many miles away.

So the first rule is simple: if you ever see a sudden, blinding flash, don't look toward it.

## Chapter 2: The first ten seconds

Inside the fireball, everything is vaporized: buildings, soil, water. That vapor and dust rush upward, and that's what forms the mushroom cloud.

The heat reaches out much farther than the fireball itself. For a bomb this size, severe burns and fires can reach about a mile. Clothing and skin facing the blast take the worst of it.

But the blast wave is slower than the light. Depending on how far away you are, it can take anywhere from a few seconds to half a minute or more to arrive. And that gap is your chance.

Official guidance says: if you're outside, drop. Lie face down behind anything solid, cover your exposed skin, and stay down until the blast and the heat have passed. If you're driving, stop safely and duck down inside the car.

## Chapter 3: The first minute

Then the blast wave hits.

Emergency planners draw it as rings. In the inner ring, about half a mile across from the center, very few buildings stay standing, and very few people are expected to survive.

Out to about a mile, buildings are badly damaged: walls blown in, cars overturned, fires breaking out. Some people are trapped, and many are hurt, but many survive.

Out to about three miles, the damage is lighter, and the biggest danger is flying glass. Windows can shatter even ten miles away. That's why you stay down, and away from windows, until it passes.

Above it all, the mushroom cloud keeps climbing, for about ten minutes.

And here's the first myth. In movies, everyone dies instantly. In reality, the worst destruction is concentrated in a fairly small circle. Most people in the wider city survive the blast. What happens to them next depends on what they do in the next hour.

## Chapter 4: The first hour

Remember the dust that was sucked up into that cloud? It's now mixed with radioactive material. And it starts coming back down. This is fallout.

Near the explosion, it can begin falling within minutes. For most of the area outside the blast damage, it usually takes fifteen minutes or more. It drifts with the wind, so it falls in a long plume, not a circle, and the most dangerous part can stretch ten to twenty miles downwind.

You might even see it. The most dangerous fallout is often visible, like fine sand or table salt landing on cars and windowsills. But not seeing it doesn't mean it isn't there.

So you have a few precious minutes. And what you do with them matters more than almost anything else.

Get inside. Not a car: a car blocks very little. A basement is much better. The middle of a large brick or concrete building, or an underground garage, is better still. The idea is simple: put as much material, and as much distance, between you and the outside as you can.

How much does this matter? When a scientist at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory modeled a blast like this in a major U.S. city, he found that if nobody took shelter, fallout could injure or kill around 280,000 people. If people sheltered in decent buildings, that number dropped dramatically. In his words, casualties from fallout are almost entirely preventable.

## Chapter 5: The first days

Here's the good news about fallout: it fades fast.

Scientists use a rule of thumb called the seven-ten rule. For every seven times longer you wait, the radiation drops about ten times. Seven hours after the explosion, it's down to about a tenth. After two days, about one percent. After two weeks, about a tenth of one percent.

That's why the official advice is to stay inside for at least twenty-four hours, unless officials tell you something different. Close the windows. Turn off fans and air conditioning that pull air in from outside. Bring pets inside too.

If you were caught outside before you got in, take off your outer layer of clothing. That alone can remove up to ninety percent of the radioactive material. Seal it in a bag, and put it away from people. Then shower with soap and shampoo, but no conditioner, because it can make radioactive particles stick to your hair. And don't scrub hard.

Food and drinks in sealed containers, and unspoiled food in your fridge or freezer, are safe after you wipe off the packaging. Bottled water is best.

And listen. A battery or hand-crank radio will still work. Officials will tell you when it's safe to leave, and which way to go.

Which brings us to the second myth: that one bomb makes the whole planet radioactive. It doesn't. The dangerous fallout is regional, it falls in one direction, and it loses most of its strength within days.

## Chapter 6: Weeks to months

For people who did receive a large dose of radiation, the danger arrives later.

Radiation sickness often starts within hours: nausea, vomiting, and feeling weak. Then something strange happens. Many people start to feel better. Doctors call it the latent phase, and it can last days or weeks. Meanwhile, the radiation has damaged the bone marrow, which makes our blood cells. When those cells run low, the body struggles to fight infection and to stop bleeding. The bigger the dose, the sooner and harder this hits.

Meanwhile, the city itself is cut off in the middle. Roads near the center are blocked by rubble. Power, water and phones are down. But the damage has a limit. Beyond about five miles, most hospitals, fire stations and power systems are only lightly affected. Help comes from the outside in, as the fallout fades and rescuers can move closer.

## Chapter 7: Years later

What about the long term?

Much of what we know comes from the survivors of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. Scientists have followed more than a hundred thousand of them for over seventy years.

Radiation did raise their cancer risk. But the numbers may surprise you. Among the survivors in the study, about one in ten solid cancers was linked to the bombs' radiation. Most of their cancers had the same causes as anyone else's.

And then there's the biggest question: what if it weren't one bomb, but many? Some climate scientists warn that huge fires could send so much smoke into the sky that it would cool the planet and devastate harvests. They call it nuclear winter. Their models suggest even a limited war could cause worldwide famine. Other researchers, including a team at Los Alamos National Laboratory, argue that much less smoke would reach the upper atmosphere. Scientists still disagree on how bad it would be.

But that's a question about many bombs. A single explosion would be a terrible disaster for one region. It would not end the world.

## Chapter 8: The city comes back

Hiroshima was destroyed on August 6, 1945. And then something remarkable happened.

Two days later, the Bank of Japan's Hiroshima branch, a sturdy building just a few hundred meters from the center, reopened its doors, so people could get cash. One day after that, a few of the city's streetcars were running again.

Today, Hiroshima is home to more than a million people.

Hiroshima and Nagasaki are not just stories about destruction. They're proof that people survive, help each other, and rebuild.

So if you remember just one thing from this video, make it this. If it ever happens: get inside, stay inside, stay tuned. Those three steps could save more lives than anything else.
