### Narration

**[0:00, cold open] VISUAL: tourists on a boardwalk, then the July 23, 2024 explosion footage (use USGS or NPS footage with credit).**

> On July 23rd, 2024, visitors at Yellowstone's Biscuit Basin, about two miles from Old Faithful, heard a sudden blast. A pool of water had turned to steam in an instant, throwing rock and mud across the boardwalk.
>
> Nobody was hurt. The boardwalk was wrecked. And the internet immediately asked the same question it always asks: is this it? Is the supervolcano waking up?
>
> It wasn't. The USGS said plainly that this kind of explosion is not caused by magma rising, and is not a sign of an impending eruption.
>
> So today we're going to do two things. First, answer the big question: what would happen if Yellowstone really erupted, tomorrow? Then I'll show you what Yellowstone is actually doing, which is a lot less cinematic, and a lot more likely.

**[1:00, Chapter 1: A caldera, not a cone] VISUAL: map zoom to the park, then a cutaway of the caldera.**

> Yellowstone isn't a cone-shaped mountain like you'd draw as a kid. It's a caldera, a huge basin left behind when an enormous eruption emptied the magma below and the ground collapsed into the space.
>
> That's what makes it a supervolcano: not a bigger bang from one peak, but a system capable of eruptions on a scale the modern world has never seen.

**[1:50, Chapter 2: Three giant eruptions] VISUAL: a timeline.**

> The U.S. Geological Survey, the USGS, has traced three of these giant caldera-forming eruptions in Yellowstone's past: about 2.1 million years ago, 1.3 million years ago, and about 640,000 years ago.

**[2:30, Chapter 3: The odds, and two myths] VISUAL: a timeline, three marks, then today.**

> So, is it due? That's the question people really want answered.
>
> The USGS says the chance of a caldera-forming eruption in any given century, or even any given thousand years, is exceedingly low. And Yellowstone's volcano alert level is Normal.
>
> You'll sometimes see someone quote an exact number, like one in 730,000 per year. Those figures aren't official USGS estimates. The honest version is: very, very unlikely.
>
> Myth one: "it's overdue." Those three eruptions aren't a clock. The gaps are about 800,000 years and then about 660,000 years, and nothing in them says a fourth is scheduled.
>
> Myth two: "there's a giant lake of magma ready to go." In 2015, University of Utah scientists mapped the upper magma chamber and found it's only about nine percent molten rock, the rest being solid. Later studies have put estimates higher, around 16 to 20 percent in one, and about 14 percent in another. But the researchers in these studies say the same thing: that's well below the roughly 35 to 50 percent usually associated with an eruptible body, and none of these studies found one.
>
> More melt isn't the same as more risk. That's what the scientists keep saying.

*[Check: "35 to 50 percent" and the later percentages come from news coverage of the studies, not the papers. **[VERIFY]** against the 2015 Utah paper, the Maguire study, and the 2025 Nature study before quoting any percentage on screen. The "800,000 and 660,000 year gaps" are arithmetic from 2.1M, 1.3M and 0.64M.]*

**[4:10, Chapter 4: If it did happen: the ash] VISUAL: the USGS ash map, labeled "Model result, not a prediction", then an ash-depth ladder graphic.**

> Okay. Let's say it happens anyway. What can scientists actually model? Where the ash goes.
>
> In 2014, USGS scientists Larry Mastin, Alexa Van Eaton, and Jacob Lowenstern published a model of what a modern Yellowstone supereruption would do. They used a computer model called Ash3d.
>
> The result looks like a bullseye. For cities within about 500 kilometers, like Billings, Montana, and Casper, Wyoming, the model shows from several centimeters to more than a meter of ash. Farther out, the layers thin quickly. In the model's example, even New York City, Los Angeles, and Miami see ash measured in millimeters.
>
> That sounds small. Here's why it isn't. The USGS says an ashfall of just one millimeter, about 1/32 of an inch, can seriously reduce visibility and make roads and tracks slippery. Airports can shut down at even thinner amounts. And power outages are common in ashfall areas.
>
> Closer in, it's weight. A dry layer of ash four inches thick weighs 120 to 200 pounds per square yard, and wet ash can weigh twice that. Flat roofs are more likely to collapse than steep ones.
>
> And remember: the exact map depends on the wind. Change the wind, and the map changes.

**[5:30, Chapter 5: Beyond the ash: the climate question] VISUAL: a sulfur haze animation in the upper atmosphere, then a cooling curve labeled "typical eruption, not Yellowstone".**

> The ash isn't actually the biggest question. Ash falls out within days to weeks. What lingers is sulfur.
>
> A big eruption can put sulfur dioxide high into the stratosphere, where it forms a thin haze of sulfuric acid droplets that reflects sunlight back to space. Up there, there's no rain to wash it out, so the cooling can last one to three years.
>
> The best-documented modern example is Mount Pinatubo in 1991, which cooled the planet by about half a degree Celsius for around two years, according to one summary.
>
> A Yellowstone supereruption would be far bigger than Pinatubo. How much cooler is genuinely uncertain. One study of ocean-floor sediment found that after Yellowstone's eruption around 630,000 years ago, the cooling lasted longer than simple climate models predicted. Another found that a huge unknown is just how big the sulfur droplets get.
>
> So, if anyone gives you a precise number for "volcanic winter" in a Yellowstone eruption, they're guessing. What scientists will say is that it would matter globally, for at least a few years.

*[Do NOT quote a temperature drop or a crop-failure figure for Yellowstone. Pinatubo's 0.5 C is from one secondary summary. **[VERIFY]** with USGS or NASA before showing it. The "630,000 years ago sediment study" is from an EarthSky summary; check the paper.]*

**[6:50, Chapter 6: What really happens at Yellowstone] VISUAL: Biscuit Basin again, now from above; a hydrothermal explosion diagram: hot water flashing to steam.**

> So here's the payoff I promised. What actually happens at Yellowstone?
>
> Hydrothermal explosions. The USGS says they're the most frequent hazard at Yellowstone, and the least destructive, compared with big earthquakes, lava flows, and caldera-forming eruptions. On average, a few of them happen somewhere in the park every year, often in the backcountry, where nobody notices.
>
> The mechanism is simple. Hot water underground is under pressure. If that pressure drops suddenly, the water flashes to steam, and the ground above blows apart. That's what happened at Black Diamond Pool in Biscuit Basin in 2024. And in September 1989, at Porkchop Geyser, in one of the park's most famous explosions.
>
> No magma needed. And after Biscuit Basin, the USGS warned that more small explosions there were possible for days to months, and the park closed the area for the rest of the season.
>
> That's the real Yellowstone: not a movie ending, but a boardwalk you have to stay on.

**[8:10, Chapter 7: How we'd know first] VISUAL: seismograph traces, GPS station, map of the monitoring network.**

> And a supereruption wouldn't come out of nowhere. The Yellowstone Volcano Observatory, a partnership of the USGS, Yellowstone National Park, and the University of Utah, watches the whole system.
>
> About 50 seismometers listen for earthquakes. Yellowstone normally has about 1,500 to 2,500 of them a year, nearly all too small to feel. GPS stations track the ground rising and falling. And gas monitors sample what's coming out of the vents.
>
> Right now, those GPS stations show the ground slowly sinking, about an inch or two a year since 2015, which is normal behavior for this system.
>
> A YVO scientist has said what a real warning would look like: thousands of earthquakes, plus uplift across the park. Not one tremor, not one explosion.

*[Do NOT say "weeks to months of warning": no source found. The YVO quote is from a news/blog piece; **[VERIFY]** against YVO's own wording.]*

**[9:00, Close and action]**

> So, what if Yellowstone erupted tomorrow? It would be among the worst natural disasters in human history. And it is exceedingly unlikely, today or in any century you or I will live in.
>
> The thing that's actually going to happen is smaller, noisier, and closer to the boardwalk.
>
> If you want to check on Yellowstone yourself, the YVO posts regular updates. Look at the alert level. It says Normal. And if that ever changes, you'll hear it from them first.
>
> Next up: a fault that scientists take very seriously, with real numbers behind it. Cascadia.

### Narration

**[0:00, cold open] VISUAL: a calm Pacific Northwest morning: ferries, coffee, fog on the water.**

> It's an ordinary morning in the Pacific Northwest. Ferries are crossing. Kids are heading to school.
>
> And then the ground starts to move. It doesn't stop in a few seconds. It keeps going.
>
> This is what scientists call a Cascadia earthquake. And it isn't science fiction. It has happened before.
>
> Most videos on this stop at the shaking. But one of the things that turns a disaster into a months-long crisis isn't the shaking, or even the wave. It's a trap, one sentence from an Oregon transportation official, and I'll show you exactly where it hides, later in this video.

**[0:50, Chapter 1: The fault] VISUAL: animated plate cutaway: Juan de Fuca plate sliding under North America.**

> Just off the coast, a piece of the Pacific sea floor, the Juan de Fuca plate, is slowly sliding under North America. Where they meet is called the Cascadia subduction zone.
>
> NOAA puts it at about 700 miles long, running from Cape Mendocino in Northern California to northern Vancouver Island in Canada, mostly offshore.

> The plates don't slide smoothly. They stick, and pressure builds. Eventually the stuck section lets go, all at once, and that's the earthquake.

*[**CONFIRMED from NOAA JetStream page you supplied:** "lies mostly off shore and extends approximately 700 miles (1,100 kilometers) from Cape Mendocino in Northern California to Northern Vancouver Island, Canada."]*

**[2:00, Chapter 2: The proof] VISUAL: Japanese historical record, wave sweeping a coastline.**

> How do we know it's happened? Because of a tsunami that struck Japan with no earthquake to explain it.
>
> In January of the year 1700, a wave about six to ten feet high hit Japan's Honshu coast. Nobody there felt any shaking. Japanese records called it the "orphan tsunami", a wave with no parent earthquake.
>
> Researchers later connected it to Cascadia, and used those records, and how long a wave takes to cross the Pacific, to pin the quake to the night of January 26th, at about nine o'clock. It's estimated at about magnitude 9.
>
> And it's not a one-off. According to NOAA, geologic evidence shows more than 40 earthquakes above magnitude 8 along this zone over the last 10,000 years.

**[3:15, Chapter 2b: The land itself remembers] VISUAL: coastal marsh, buried soil layers diagram. *Do NOT use the Neskowin ghost forest as proof (see note).***

> The land itself keeps a record. In the 1980s and 1990s, scientists doing fieldwork found evidence that coastal land in the Northwest had dropped suddenly in the past, and was then flooded by tsunami waves and mud.
>
> That's what happens when a stuck plate lets go: the coast can sink in an instant.

*[Note: Oregon's "ghost forests", like the one at Neskowin, are often shown as proof of this. One account says not all geologists agree that forest was killed by an earthquake, and some credit sea-level change and migrating sand. Leave ghost-forest footage out, or say "some" and mention the dispute, until a paleoseismology source is checked. **[VERIFY]**]*

**[3:30, Chapter 3: The odds] VISUAL: simple bar showing 10-15%, labeled "next 50 years".**

> So how likely is it? Here's the careful answer.
>
> A recent USGS fact sheet puts the chance of a roughly magnitude 9 earthquake along the whole zone at about 10 to 15 percent over the next 50 years. About 10 percent if you assume earthquakes happen at random, and about 15 percent if you factor in that the last big one was in 1700.
>
> You'll see higher numbers, like one in three, in older work. Those cover a broader group of large earthquakes, not just a full magnitude 9 rupture. They're not the same question.
>
> What does that mean? Not tomorrow, and not "overdue". Just a real, serious chance in the lifetime of a child born today.

*[**USGS method confirmed from the fact-sheet page you supplied:** USGS computes these as chances over 50 years, using a time-independent ("Poissonian") model, P = 1 - e^(-t/tau), in which an earthquake is equally likely on any day. USGS itself says that assumption "is likely an incorrect assumption for individual faults" but a reasonable way to compute hazard where past dates are uncertain. Your screenshot did not show the 10-15% numbers themselves; they still come from the earlier search summary of the 2025 fact sheet. **[VERIFY]** the exact percentages and the phrase "about 15 percent if you factor in the 1700 date" on the fact sheet before recording.]*

**[4:40, Chapter 4: The shaking] VISUAL: shaking POV in an office, a summer beach, a long seismograph ribbon, a home shaking. (The 3:00 timer idea was dropped.)**

> Now, back to our morning. What would the shaking feel like?
>
> The last one struck around nine o'clock on a late January night in 1700. The authors of Oregon's own resilience plan point out the next one could just as easily shake a mid-July morning, with hundreds of thousands of Oregonians and visitors at the coast. No one can predict it.
>
> The key difference from a typical California quake is how long it lasts. State geologists in Oregon have said shaking could last several minutes. Some of their briefings have said three to five.
>
> Several minutes. That's long enough to be frightening, long enough to damage buildings, and long enough that you'd want to know exactly what to do.

**[5:40, Chapter 5: Early warning] VISUAL: sensors, a phone alert, two animated waves racing.**

> Could your phone warn you first? Sometimes, yes. There's a system called ShakeAlert, run by the USGS.
>
> It doesn't predict earthquakes. It detects one that has already started, and works out where it is and how big, within seconds. Then it sends an alert, and the alert travels almost instantly, much faster than the shaking itself, which moves at just a few kilometers per second.
>
> That's a gap measured in seconds, and seconds are enough to Drop, Cover, and Hold On, or to let automated systems act. The USGS lists California, Oregon, and Washington as covered states.
>
> But there's an honest limit. For every earthquake, there's a zone near the epicenter where the alert arrives after the shaking has started. People close to it may get little or no warning.

*[Do NOT say how many seconds Cascadia would give anyone. Cascadia's rupture is offshore, so warning time on the coast vs. inland differs, but no sourced figure was found. **[VERIFY]**]*

**[7:00, Chapter 6: The wave] VISUAL: coastline, a siren, a mapped evacuation route; no gore. On screen: a "YOUR WINDOW" countdown bar from 30:00 down to 10:00, filling red as the narration gives each estimate.**

> Here's where the coast is different from everywhere else.
>
> Right after the shaking stops, a tsunami heads for the shore. Estimates for the Oregon coast range from roughly 10 to 30 minutes after the shaking begins, depending on the source and the place.
>
> In Washington, a state assessment of the outer coast, built on walk-time estimates for a magnitude 9 quake, found that people may have only 10 to 15 minutes once the ground stops shaking to reach high ground.
>
> And here's the problem. Along many parts of Washington's outer coast, the same assessment says it can take an hour or more to walk to high ground, or out of the tsunami zone. Ten to fifteen minutes of warning, an hour of walking.
>
> Take the city of Seaside, Oregon. The resilience plan cites a study finding that 83 percent of its population, 89 percent of its employees, and almost all of its critical facilities sit inside the tsunami zone.
>
> And in some places, there isn't any high ground close enough. So Washington has started building towers. In 2016, a school near Westport called Ocosta Elementary unveiled the first tsunami vertical evacuation structure in North America: a place to climb, instead of somewhere to run to. The state's assessment found that dozens more are needed on the outer coast.
>
> The sources disagree a little on exact times, so treat them as a window, not a countdown. Check the evacuation map where you live.
>
> Now let's say you made it to high ground. You're alive. The shaking has stopped, the wave has passed. And this is where the trap I mentioned closes.

*[**CONFIRMED from the Washington Emergency Management Division page you supplied:** the assessment (by the University of Washington's Institute for Hazards Mitigation Planning and Research, funded by a NOAA grant, covering Pacific, Grays Harbor and Clallam counties) used walk-time estimates for a magnitude 9.0 Cascadia quake; "people on the outer coast may only have 10 to 15 minutes once the ground stops shaking to reach high ground before a tsunami arrives"; "along many parts of Washington's outer coast, it can take an hour or more to walk to high ground or out of the tsunami inundation zone"; dozens of vertical evacuation structures are needed. **[8:30, Chapter 7: After the quake] VISUAL: damaged bridge illustration, fuel gauge, empty tap; label "illustration". When the recovery times are read, show an on-screen table, "If it happened today", from the plan's recovery table.**

> Here it is. A 2019 Oregon Department of Transportation presentation put it in one sentence: you can't get fuel until the bridges are fixed, but you can't fix the bridges without fuel.
>
> Think about what that means. Most of the bridges in western Oregon are susceptible to serious damage. Oregon gets almost all of its liquid fuel from suppliers in Washington, by pipeline and river, a state that sits on the same fault. And once it arrives, the fuel is stored in a single six-mile stretch of the lower Willamette River, on riverside soil that can turn liquid when it shakes.
>
> That's why the 2013 Oregon Resilience Plan, written for the state legislature, began with a blunt finding: Oregon is far from resilient to a great Cascadia earthquake and tsunami today.
>
> Its numbers: available studies estimate deaths from 1,250 to more than 10,000, from the shaking and the tsunami combined. Tens of thousands of buildings destroyed or damaged so badly they'd need months to years of repair. Tens of thousands of households displaced. More than 30 billion dollars in losses, close to a fifth of the state's economy. And more than a million dump-truck loads of debris.
>
> And here's how long recovery would take under today's conditions. Electricity: one to three months in the Willamette Valley, three to six months on the coast. Police and fire stations in the valley: two to four months. Drinking water and sewer: one month to a year in the valley, one to three years on the coast. Hospitals: about a year and a half in the valley, and three years on the coast.
>
> The plan says businesses usually plan to survive about two weeks without essential services. Under present conditions, it warns, outages could last much longer, in some cases 18 to 36 months or more.
>
> Now compare that with what other countries did. After Japan's magnitude 9.0 earthquake in 2011, more than 90 percent of the power was back in ten days, 90 percent of phone lines in two weeks, and 90 percent of cell stations in 19 days. After Chile's magnitude 8.8 quake in 2010, 95 percent of power returned within two weeks.
>
> That gap, between months to years and days to weeks, is what Oregon's plan was written to close.

**[10:00, Chapter 8: Schools and old buildings] VISUAL: a brick school building, seismic retrofit work; label "illustration" unless real footage is licensed.**

> But the story doesn't end in that trap. Here's a number that shows what's possible.
>
> In 2013, Oregon's resilience plan reported that the state's seismic grant program had retrofitted 22 schools. That sounded like a start. It was about two percent of the need the state had documented.
>

>

>
> The hardest buildings to fix are unreinforced masonry: brick or block with nothing holding it together inside. They're the most vulnerable in an earthquake and among the most expensive to retrofit. Portland has the biggest concentration in the state, and in 2019 Oregon created a separate program for those non-school buildings.
>
> So: a fault that can't be stopped, and a state slowly, expensively making what's on top of it stronger.
